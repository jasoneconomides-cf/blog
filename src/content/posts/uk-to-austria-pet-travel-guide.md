Taking a dog, cat or ferret from Great Britain to Austria usually means arranging a microchip, a valid rabies vaccination and an animal health certificate before you leave. Austria is in the EU, but if you travel overland your pet normally enters the EU in another country first. That first border crossing is an important part of the plan.

For the journey home, dogs travelling from Austria to England, Scotland or Wales normally need a vet-administered tapeworm treatment 24 to 120 hours before arrival. Cats and ferrets do not need that treatment.

This guide is for ordinary holidays and other non-commercial journeys from England, Scotland or Wales. Northern Ireland has different pet-travel arrangements, and different rules apply if a pet is being sold, rehomed or travelling commercially.

## Austria pet travel checklist

For a typical non-commercial trip, work through these essentials:

1. Make sure your pet's microchip can be read and its number matches the veterinary records.
2. Check that the rabies vaccination was given after the microchip was implanted or read.
3. Allow the required waiting period after a first rabies vaccination.
4. Book an Official Veterinarian to issue an animal health certificate.
5. Confirm where you will first enter the EU and what your carrier requires.
6. Check the pet rules for every country on your route, not only Austria.
7. Reserve pet-friendly transport and accommodation in advance.
8. If returning with a dog, arrange tapeworm treatment in the correct 24-to-120-hour window.
9. Return on an approved pet route and keep the original documents accessible.

## Which pets does this guide cover?

The standard EU pet-travel rules covered here apply to dogs, cats and ferrets accompanying their owner on a private journey. GOV.UK currently allows up to five pets on an animal health certificate.

There are limited exceptions for certain events, such as competitions or sporting events. If you are travelling with more than five animals, transferring ownership, selling a pet or transporting an animal for a commercial purpose, do not assume the ordinary pet scheme applies.

Birds, rabbits, rodents, reptiles and other species have different import requirements. Check the relevant Austrian authority and any transit country before booking.

## Microchip and rabies vaccination rules

Your dog, cat or ferret must be microchipped before, or at the same time as, its rabies vaccination. If the vaccination happened first, GOV.UK says the pet will need to be vaccinated again.

For a first rabies vaccination, your pet must be old enough to receive the vaccine and you must wait for it to become valid before travelling. GOV.UK says this will be at least 21 full days after the first vaccination, although the vaccine used may require longer. Day one of the waiting period is the day after vaccination.

A booster normally avoids a new waiting period if there has been no break in rabies cover. Check the vaccination date, product validity and microchip number well before paying for travel. If the rabies vaccination expires during the trip, the animal health certificate cannot be used for further EU travel or re-entry to Great Britain.

## Do I need an Animal Health Certificate for Austria?

If you live in England, Scotland or Wales, plan to obtain an animal health certificate for each separate trip to Austria. Current GOV.UK guidance says GB residents cannot use an EU-issued pet passport instead of an AHC to enter the EU. A passport issued in Great Britain is also no longer valid for EU entry.

The AHC must be signed by an Official Veterinarian. Your usual practice can issue it if an authorised OV works there; otherwise, ask where you can find one. Our guides explain [how an animal health certificate works](/posts/animal-health-certificate-guide/) and [whether a normal vet can issue an AHC](/posts/can-normal-vet-issue-animal-health-certificate/).

Take evidence of the microchipping date and rabies vaccination history to the appointment. Check every microchip number, date and owner detail before leaving the practice.

The certificate is currently valid for:

- entry to the EU within 10 days of issue
- onward travel within the EU for up to six months after entry
- re-entry to Great Britain for up to six months

Those periods also end if the recorded rabies vaccination expires sooner. Up to five pets can appear on one certificate.

## Your first EU border may not be Austria

Most road and rail journeys from Great Britain reach France, Belgium or the Netherlands before Austria. That means the relevant EU arrival point is where your pet first enters the EU, not the Austrian border later in the journey.

For example, a family driving through the Channel Tunnel and continuing through France and Germany enters the EU in France. The pet paperwork is presented for the EU-entry checks there; the same valid AHC then covers onward movement to Austria during that trip.

Use a suitable travellers' point of entry and tell the vet issuing the certificate which country you will enter first. Check that the planned crossing accepts pets and confirm its current check-in procedure with the carrier. If you change from a French route to a Dutch or Belgian route after the certificate is prepared, contact the issuing vet rather than assuming the paperwork is still right for the itinerary.

Our [UK-to-France dog travel guide](/posts/uk-to-france-dog-after-brexit/) explains the common first leg through France. You must also check the rules and carrier conditions for every transit country.

## Does Austria require tapeworm treatment on arrival?

Austria does not normally require the special *Echinococcus multilocularis* treatment that GOV.UK lists for dogs entering Finland, Ireland, Malta, Norway or Northern Ireland.

That is separate from the rule for returning to Great Britain. A dog travelling home from Austria normally does need tapeworm treatment before arriving in England, Scotland or Wales.

## Travelling to Austria by car

Driving offers control over rest stops, temperature and luggage, but it is a long journey. Your plan should include:

- a secure carrier or appropriate in-car restraint
- water, food, medication and familiar bedding
- regular safe breaks suitable for your animal
- a spare lead and copies of booking details
- an overnight stop if the journey would otherwise be too demanding
- access to the original AHC throughout the journey

Research pet rules for the UK crossing, France, Belgium or the Netherlands, Germany and any other country used. Toll systems, road equipment and driving requirements are separate from pet-entry rules and can change independently.

Do not leave a pet in a parked vehicle. Mountain and city temperatures can change quickly, and a car can become unsafe even when the weather does not feel particularly hot.

## Flying to Austria with a pet

An airline's acceptance policy is separate from the legal entry rules. Before booking, confirm:

- whether your species, breed, size and number of pets are accepted
- whether the animal travels in the cabin, hold or as cargo
- the carrier dimensions and construction required
- the airport's pet handling and document-check process
- any health or fitness-to-fly paperwork the airline requires

A flight being available for people does not mean the airline accepts pets on that route. The rules for entering Great Britain by air are also different: pets normally travel as cargo unless they are recognised assistance dogs or are travelling on a chartered private plane. Check the current approved return carriers before committing to a round trip.

## Getting around Austria with a dog

Austria's leash and muzzle rules are not identical nationwide. The Austrian government says requirements can be set by individual states and municipalities, so the rule may change between Vienna, Salzburg, Tyrol and a rural resort.

On Austrian public transport, small animals can generally travel in a suitable closed container. Dogs are commonly required to be on a lead and wear a muzzle, and a reduced ticket may be needed. Check the exact conditions with the railway or local transport operator before travelling.

Pack a properly fitted muzzle and practise using it calmly before the holiday, even if you expect to drive. Also check local rules for parks, lakes, cable cars, protected areas and busy public places. Accommodation marked “pet friendly” may still limit animal size, numbers or access to restaurants and communal areas, so get the terms in writing.

## Veterinary planning in Austria

Save the details of a veterinary practice near your accommodation before leaving the UK. If you are travelling with a dog, ask whether it can provide the treatment and complete the relevant section of your AHC for the return journey.

Carry enough regular medication for the trip and discuss ongoing conditions with your own vet. Pet insurance and breakdown cover are not substitutes for the legal paperwork, but suitable cover can help with unexpected veterinary care or disrupted travel. Check exclusions, territorial limits and assistance arrangements rather than relying on the policy name alone.

## Returning from Austria to Great Britain

For a return to England, Scotland or Wales, your pet must travel with an accepted document. A GB-issued AHC used to enter the EU can normally be used for the return during its validity period, provided the rabies vaccination remains valid.

Dogs coming from Austria normally need a vet to administer tapeworm treatment:

- no less than 24 hours before arrival in Great Britain
- no more than 120 hours, or five days, before arrival

The vet must record the product name and manufacturer, the exact date and time, and add their stamp and signature. Count the window back from the scheduled arrival in Great Britain, not from leaving Austria or boarding the ferry.

Read our full guide to [dog tapeworm treatment for returning to Great Britain](/posts/dog-tapeworm-treatment-returning-great-britain/) before booking the appointment. If a delay would take the journey outside the treatment window, contact the carrier and a vet promptly.

You must also use a route and carrier approved for pets entering Great Britain. Approved routes and operating conditions can change, so recheck the live GOV.UK list shortly before travelling.

## A practical planning timeline

### Several weeks or months before travel

- Have the microchip scanned and verify the number in the records.
- Check the rabies vaccination and allow time for any required waiting period.
- Compare routes and confirm pet acceptance in both directions.
- Check local Austrian rules and book genuinely pet-friendly accommodation.
- Locate an Official Veterinarian for the AHC.

### Within the AHC entry window

- Attend the OV appointment with your pet and supporting records.
- Check all entries before leaving.
- Make sure the EU entry date falls within the certificate's validity period.

### Before leaving Austria

- For a dog, arrange tapeworm treatment 24 to 120 hours before arrival in Great Britain.
- Check the vet has recorded the exact time and all required details.
- Reconfirm the carrier, departure and approved return route.
- Keep the original travel document ready for checks.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Treating Austria as the first EU entry country when the journey enters through France, Belgium or the Netherlands.
- Booking transport before checking whether that exact service accepts pets.
- Discovering too late that the rabies vaccination was recorded before the microchip.
- Counting the AHC's 10-day window from the wrong date.
- Assuming Austrian leash and muzzle rules are the same in every municipality.
- Forgetting that a dog needs return tapeworm treatment even though Austria does not normally require it on arrival.
- Timing treatment from the departure time rather than arrival in Great Britain.
- Letting the rabies vaccination expire during a long trip.

## Final check before you book

A well-planned Austria trip is perfectly manageable, but it involves three layers: EU entry rules, the rules of each carrier and transit country, and the requirements for returning to Great Britain. Build the itinerary first, then arrange the veterinary paperwork around the actual borders and arrival times.

This article provides general information, not personalised veterinary or legal advice. Rules and carrier procedures can change. Check GOV.UK, the European Commission, the Austrian authorities, your vet and every travel operator before departure.
