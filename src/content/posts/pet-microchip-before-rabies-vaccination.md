Yes. For a dog, cat or ferret travelling from Great Britain to the EU, the pet must be microchipped **before, or at the same appointment before, its rabies vaccination is given**. The order matters because the microchip number connects the vaccination record and travel document to that particular animal.

If the rabies vaccination was given before the pet was microchipped or its existing chip was read, current GOV.UK guidance says the pet will need to be vaccinated again. The required waiting period then applies before travel.

This is a small detail with a large effect on a travel timetable, so check it before booking an Animal Health Certificate appointment or non-refundable transport.

## Why must the microchip come first?

The microchip is the pet's permanent identifier. A vet records its unique number alongside the rabies vaccination details, allowing a carrier or border checker to connect:

- the animal presented for travel
- the microchip number read from that animal
- the recorded rabies vaccination
- the Animal Health Certificate or other accepted travel document

EU rules say the date of the rabies vaccination must not be earlier than the date the pet was identified by microchip implantation or reading. It is not enough for a pet to have both a chip and a current vaccination if the records do not establish the correct sequence.

## Can microchipping and vaccination happen on the same day?

Yes, provided the microchip is implanted or read **before** the rabies vaccine is administered. The vet should scan the chip, confirm its number and record the details accurately before vaccinating.

Because both events may show the same calendar date, the clinical records need to make the order clear. Ask the practice to check that the microchip number and relevant date are present in the vaccination record you will later show to the Official Veterinarian.

## What if my pet was vaccinated before being microchipped?

GOV.UK says the pet will need another rabies vaccination after microchipping. That later vaccination becomes the one used for the pet-travel requirements.

If it is treated as a first or primary vaccination for travel, you must wait for it to become valid before entering the EU. GOV.UK says this will be at least 21 full days, although the vaccine used may require a longer wait. Day one is the day after vaccination.

Do not try to calculate a new departure date from internet advice alone. Ask the vet administering the vaccine and the Official Veterinarian issuing the AHC to confirm the valid-from date for the product and records involved.

## What if the pet was already chipped, but the records are unclear?

This is different from knowing that vaccination happened first. The pet may have been correctly microchipped, but the implantation date or earlier clinical record might be difficult to find.

Ask your veterinary practice to search the original clinical history and any transferred records. Useful evidence may include:

- the original microchip implantation record
- an earlier vaccination entry containing the same chip number
- a dated record showing that a vet scanned and verified the chip
- previous travel documents or certified supporting records

The Official Veterinarian must decide whether the evidence is sufficient to complete the AHC correctly. Do not add dates to records yourself or assume that a microchip database registration date proves when the chip was implanted.

If the correct sequence cannot be established, the vet may advise scanning the chip, vaccinating again and restarting the travel timetable. Resolve this early enough to accommodate the full waiting period.

## Does the vet scan the chip for an Animal Health Certificate?

The microchip must be verified by a vet before its details are entered on a travel certificate. At the AHC appointment, the OV will examine the animal and check the supporting microchip and vaccination evidence.

Take the original records requested by the practice. Before leaving, compare the chip number on the certificate with the supporting vaccination record. A long number is easy to mistype, and this is the best moment to correct a clerical error.

## Will the microchip be scanned during the journey?

Your pet's identity and documents may be checked when entering the EU and when returning to Great Britain. The process varies by route and operator, but owners should expect the chip to be readable and the scanned number to match the travel document.

Do not rely on reports that someone else's pet was not scanned. A journey with fewer visible checks does not change the legal requirements for the next traveller.

Keep the original travel document accessible rather than buried in luggage. Follow the carrier's instructions about where the animal must be presented and allow enough time for pet check-in.

## What if the microchip cannot be read?

Have your vet scan the chip well before travel. GOV.UK says that if the vet cannot read it, the preparation may need to be done again, including:

- implanting a new microchip
- giving a new rabies vaccination
- issuing a new AHC where required
- waiting the applicable period before travelling

This is why a pre-travel scan several weeks before departure is safer than discovering a problem during the final AHC appointment.

If a chip can only sometimes be read, GOV.UK says the vet should try to obtain a reading and may insert a second chip without removing the original. Both numbers and the relevant reading and implantation dates must be recorded in the AHC's identification section, with the vet's signature and stamp.

Follow your vet's advice for the individual animal. Do not ask for a working chip to be removed simply to simplify the paperwork.

## Does the chip need to meet a particular standard?

GOV.UK says airlines, train operators and ferry companies in the EU can read chips meeting ISO standards 11784 and 11785. If a chip does not meet those standards, you may need to provide a compatible reader yourself.

Ask your vet what kind of chip your pet has and check the carrier's current requirements before travel. An overseas or older chip is not automatically unusable, but its compatibility must be dealt with in advance.

There is a narrow alternative for a clearly legible identification tattoo applied on or before 3 July 2011, subject to the official conditions. Most owners should plan around a readable microchip.

## Is registering the microchip enough?

No. Database registration and pet-travel identification are related but separate matters.

Keeping the database contact details current can help reunite you with a missing pet. It does not replace:

- proof that the chip was implanted or read before vaccination
- a valid rabies vaccination
- an AHC or another accepted travel document
- any route-specific or return requirements

Check the database record as part of your planning, but take the veterinary evidence requested by the OV.

## What details should I check in the vaccination record?

GOV.UK says the pet's vaccination record must include details such as:

- the pet's date of birth
- the microchip number, the date it was implanted or read, and its location on the animal
- the vaccination date
- the vaccine manufacturer, product name and batch number
- the date the vaccination becomes valid for travel
- the date the vaccination remains valid until
- the vet's signature and contact details

Check that the same microchip number appears consistently throughout. Also check for a break in rabies coverage: a booster given within the previous vaccine's validity usually continues the cover, but a missed booster can restart the vaccination and waiting process.

## A worked timing example

Suppose a dog has never been microchipped or vaccinated against rabies:

1. On 1 October, the vet implants and scans the microchip, then administers the rabies vaccine.
2. The vaccine used requires 21 full days before travel. Day one is 2 October.
3. The earliest travel date would therefore be 23 October.
4. The owner arranges for an OV to issue the AHC so EU entry occurs within the certificate's 10-day entry window.

That example illustrates the order of events; it is not a personalised travel calculation. Some vaccines take longer to become valid, and your actual route or destination may add requirements.

## Pre-travel microchip checklist

Several weeks before departure:

1. Ask your vet to scan the chip and confirm that it reads reliably.
2. Compare the number with every vaccination and travel record.
3. Confirm that implantation or a documented reading occurred before the vaccination relied upon for travel.
4. Check the rabies valid-until date and whether any booster was late.
5. Confirm that the chip is readable with standard equipment or plan for a compatible reader.
6. Update your microchip database contact details.
7. Leave time to repeat vaccination and the waiting period if the records or chip create a problem.

## Remember the return journey

Correct microchip and rabies records are only part of the trip. Your AHC can normally cover onward EU travel and return to Great Britain during its validity, provided the rabies vaccination does not expire first.

Dogs returning to England, Scotland or Wales from most EU countries also need vet-administered tapeworm treatment in the correct window. Our guide explains the [24-to-120-hour tapeworm rule](/posts/dog-tapeworm-treatment-returning-great-britain/).

## The short answer

Microchip first, rabies vaccination second. They can happen at the same appointment, but the chip must be implanted or read before the vaccine is given and the records must support that order. Scan the chip and audit the dates early; doing so shortly before departure leaves very little room to fix a problem.

This article is general information, not personalised veterinary or legal advice. Pet-travel rules and carrier procedures can change, so check current GOV.UK and destination guidance and speak to an appropriately authorised vet before travelling.
